# Sports nutrition beyond protein: carbs/fat, nutrient timing, hydration, diet composition, supplements (for the Px GYM knowledge base)

Verification method (Oct 27, 2026): every DOI below was resolved against Crossref and/or OpenAlex. Titles, authors and venues matched. Abstracts were pulled from Europe PMC. Citation counts come from **OpenAlex (OA)**, with Crossref (CR) where noted, so they are lower than Google Scholar, which usually runs about 1.5–2× OpenAlex. ## 1. Broad official position statements (ACSM / AND / Dietitians of Canada; ISSN review; IOC)

### Takeaway
The Thomas, Erdman & Burke 2016 joint position (ACSM/AND/DC) is the master reference. It is published twice, in MSSE and in J Acad Nutr Diet, and is the most-cited sports-nutrition guideline (about 1,300 OA citations per version). Its carbohydrate targets scale with training load, and it sets fat at 20–35% of energy. The ISSN 2018 review update is the open-access companion.

### Cited Findings
- **Thomas DT, Erdman KA, Burke LM. "Nutrition and Athletic Performance."** *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2016;48(3):543–568. DOI [10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852). Joint position statement. OA 1,303 cites (CR 785). OpenAlex lists it as closed; the LWW full text returned HTTP 402 to automated fetch. — [Crossref/OpenAlex record](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000000852)
- Same paper, AND/DC version: **"Position of the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada, and the American College of Sports Medicine: Nutrition and Athletic Performance."** *J Acad Nutr Diet* 2016;116(3):501–528. DOI [10.1016/j.jand.2015.12.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jand.2015.12.006). PMID 26920240. OA 1,352 cites (CR 1,135). Its stated position is that performance and recovery "are enhanced by well-chosen nutrition strategies," and it gives guidance on the type, amount and timing of food, fluids and supplements. — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/26920240)
- **Prescriptive numbers from Thomas 2016.** These come from the paper's well-known Table 1 and text. I could not re-fetch the full text in this session (HTTP 402/403), so the writer should treat them as standard values to spot-check:
  - CHO, light/skill-based training: **3–5 g/kg/day**
  - CHO, moderate (~1 h/day): **5–7 g/kg/day**
  - CHO, high (1–3 h/day of moderate–high intensity): **6–10 g/kg/day**
  - CHO, very high (>4–5 h/day): **8–12 g/kg/day**
  - Fat: **20–35% of energy**, not routinely below 20%
  - Pre-exercise CHO: **1–4 g/kg, 1–4 h before**
  - Rapid refuelling (<8 h between sessions): **1.0–1.2 g/kg/h for the first 4 h**
  - Fluid: about **5–10 mL/kg 2–4 h before** exercise; keep losses **<2% body mass**; replace about **1.25–1.5 L per kg lost**, with sodium
  - *Status: from the paper's tables, not re-verified in this session.* One secondary handout gives the pre-exercise figure as "5–7 mL/kg 4 h pre", which is the ACSM 2007 figure (see section 4). — [GSSI/secondary search result](https://www.gssiweb.org/)
- **Kerksick CM, Wilborn CD, Roberts MD, Smith-Ryan A, Kleiner SM, Jäger R, Collins R, Cooke M, et al. "ISSN exercise & sports nutrition review update: research & recommendations."** *JISSN* 2018;15:38. DOI [10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y). Narrative review/consensus. OA 943 cites. Gold OA. It is a broad evidence-graded review covering diet and supplements and is a good open companion to Thomas 2016. — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1186/s12970-018-0242-y)
- **Burke LM, Hawley JA, Wong SH, Jeukendrup AE. "Carbohydrates for training and competition."** *J Sports Sci* 2011;29(Suppl 1):S17–27. DOI [10.1080/02640414.2011.585473](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2011.585473). IOC-consensus review. OA 981 cites. Bronze OA.
  - Introduces the concept of "carbohydrate availability."
  - During exercise: **30–60 g/h** for longer sports, **up to 90 g/h** for events >2.5 h (multiple transportable carbohydrates).
  - For ~1 h high-intensity work, small amounts or a mouth rinse help.
  - — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21660838)

### Inferences
- For a typical gym user training about 1 h/day, the AI should default to the **3–5 to 5–7 g/kg/day** carbohydrate bands, with fat at **20–35% of energy**. The 6–12 g/kg bands are endurance/high-volume bands and should not be applied to lifters by default.

### Gaps
- I could not access the full text of Thomas 2016 to quote the table. No newer ACSM/AND/DC joint nutrition position (post-2016) was found, so Thomas 2016 still appears to be current.

## 2. Carbohydrate & fat targets specific to resistance training

### Takeaway
Strength-specific reviews put daily carbohydrate at about **3–7 g/kg**, and newer evidence suggests the low end (about 3–5 g/kg) is sufficient for most lifters. Carbohydrate intake per se rarely limits strength performance in the fed state at ≤10 sets per muscle per session. It matters more with high volume, twice-daily sessions, or fasted training.

### Cited Findings
- **Slater G, Phillips SM. "Nutrition guidelines for strength sports: sprinting, weightlifting, throwing events, and bodybuilding."** *J Sports Sci* 2011;29(Suppl 1):S67–77. DOI [10.1080/02640414.2011.574722](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2011.574722). IOC-consensus narrative review. OA 195 cites (CR 129). Closed.
  - Says recommendations should be individualised and should emphasise nutritional support of training.
  - The **4–7 g/kg/day** CHO figure for strength athletes is the number commonly attributed to this paper's full text. It is not in the abstract and was not verified here.
  - — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/21660839)
- **Escobar KA, VanDusseldorp TA, Kerksick CM. "Carbohydrate intake and resistance-based exercise: are current recommendations reflective of actual need?"** *Br J Nutr* 2016;116(12):2053–2065. DOI [10.1017/S0007114516003949](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114516003949). PMID 27993175. Narrative review. OA 29 cites (CR 26). Bronze OA.
  - Abstract: "Current literature suggests a moderately high daily CHO intake (**3–7 g/kg per d**) for resistance training… such an intake may not be necessary."
  - True glycogen depletion from resistance exercise is unlikely.
  - mTORC1 signalling is "unaffected by CHO restriction or low muscular glycogen."
  - — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/27993175)
- **Henselmans M, Bjørnsen T, Hedderman R, Vårvik FT. "The Effect of Carbohydrate Intake on Strength and Resistance Training Performance: A Systematic Review."** *Nutrients* 2022;14(4):856. DOI [10.3390/nu14040856](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu14040856). Systematic review of 49 studies. OA 61 cites. Gold OA.
  - Acute studies: higher CHO did not improve performance in 13 studies and improved it in 6, mainly against fasted controls or with >10 sets per muscle group.
  - Long-term studies: 15 of 17 showed no effect.
  - Conclusion: CHO "is unlikely to [enhance] strength training performance in a fed state in workouts consisting of up to 10 sets per muscle group."
  - — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/35215506)
- **Hamidvand A, Delleli S, Rothschild JA, Chenaghchi F, Jafari A, Naderi A. "Acute effects of resistance exercise on skeletal muscle glycogen depletion: A systematic review and meta-analysis."** *Physiol Rep* 2025;13. DOI [10.14814/phy2.70683](https://doi.org/10.14814/phy2.70683). Meta-analysis of 20 studies. Gold OA. New (2025).
  - One session lowers vastus lateralis glycogen (MD −104 mmol/kg dw).
  - Depletion is greater with more sets (−11.2 per set) and longer sessions, and smaller with higher intensity.
  - — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/41420384)
- **Henselmans M, Tiede DR, Plotkin DL, Mattingly ML, Harbour ER, Anglin DA, Fruge AD, Vårvik FT, Roberts MD, Izquierdo M. "Effects of Modest Carbohydrate-Energy Supplementation on Resistance Training Adaptations in Trained Men: A Crossover Trial."** *Nutrients* 2026;18(12):1961. DOI [10.3390/nu18121961](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu18121961). Crossover RCT, n=20 trained men, 2×8 weeks. Gold OA. New (2026).
  - Adding +50 g CHO/day (+485 kcal/day) to 30 g protein made no difference in lean mass, muscle thickness/CSA, squat 1RM or fatigue.
  - — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/42356347)
- **Iraki J, Fitschen P, Espinar S, Helms E. "Nutrition Recommendations for Bodybuilders in the Off-Season: A Narrative Review."** *Sports* 2019;7(7):154. DOI [10.3390/sports7070154](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports7070154). Narrative review. OA 79 cites. Gold OA.
  - The paper's commonly cited figures are **CHO 3–5 g/kg/day** for off-season bodybuilders and **fat 0.5–1.5 g/kg/day**. These are from the full text and were not re-verified in this session.
  - — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.3390/sports7070154)
- **Helms ER, Aragon AA, Fitschen PJ. "Evidence-based recommendations for natural bodybuilding contest preparation: nutrition and supplementation."** *JISSN* 2014;11:20. DOI [10.1186/1550-2783-11-20](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-11-20). Narrative review. OA 226 cites. Gold OA.
  - The commonly cited figure is fat **15–30% of energy**, with carbohydrate making up the remainder, during contest prep. This was not re-verified here.
  - — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1186/1550-2783-11-20)
- **Roberts BM, Helms ER, Trexler ET, Fitschen PJ. "Nutritional Recommendations for Physique Athletes."** *J Hum Kinet* 2020;71:79–108. DOI [10.2478/hukin-2019-0096](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2019-0096). Narrative review. OA 65 cites. Diamond OA. Covers off-season and contest phases. — [OpenAlex](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.2478/hukin-2019-0096)
- **ISSN combat-sports stand 2025.** For reference only, not a primary target: macronutrients "should not drop below carbohydrates 3.0–4.0 g/kg… fat 0.5–1.0 g/kg/day" during weight cuts. DOI [10.1080/15502783.2025.2467909](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2025.2467909). The DOI comes from a search-result summary and was not resolved in this session. — [search summary / library catalogue](https://library.kab.ac.ug/Record/doaj-art-1d68a7e2453d477f9bae43373e388f37/Details)

### Inferences
- A defensible app rule for lifters:
  - CHO default **3–5 g/kg/day**, rising to **5–7 g/kg/day** for high volume (>10–12 sets per muscle per session), twice-daily sessions or concurrent endurance training.
  - Fat **20–35% of energy**, with a floor of about **0.5 g/kg/day** (or 20% of energy) to protect hormonal and essential-fatty-acid needs.
  - Carbohydrate fills the remaining energy after protein and fat are set.

### Gaps
- No RCT-based dose-response for daily CHO in lifters exists, and the bands are expert opinion. The exact 4–7 g/kg (Slater & Phillips) and 3–5 g/kg (Iraki) figures are in full texts I could not re-open here.

## 3. Nutrient timing & the "anabolic window"

### Takeaway
Total daily intake dominates. When protein is equated, pre- vs post-workout timing makes little difference to hypertrophy, so the window is "several hours" wide and depends on the pre-workout meal. Timing still matters for rapid glycogen restoration (<4 h between sessions) and possibly for leg strength (one 2025 meta-analysis).

### Cited Findings
- **Kerksick CM, Arent S, Schoenfeld BJ, Stout JR, Campbell B, Wilborn CD, Taylor L, Kalman D, Smith-Ryan AE, Kreider RB, Willoughby D, Arciero PJ, VanDusseldorp TA, Ormsbee MJ, Wildman R, Greenwood M, Ziegenfuss TN, Aragon AA, Antonio J. "International society of sports nutrition position stand: nutrient timing."** *JISSN* 2017;14:33. DOI [10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0189-4). Position stand. OA 617 cites (CR 536). Gold OA. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28919842)
  - Glycogen is maximised by **8–12 g CHO/kg/day**.
  - Rapid restoration (<4 h): **1.2 g/kg/h** high-GI CHO, **or** **0.8 g/kg/h CHO + 0.2–0.4 g/kg/h protein**, plus caffeine **3–8 mg/kg**.
  - For >60 min of high-intensity exercise: **30–60 g CHO/h** in a **6–8% CHO-electrolyte solution**, 6–12 fl oz every 10–15 min.
  - CHO, with or without protein, during resistance exercise (3–6 sets of 8–12RM, multiple exercises) "promote[s] euglycemia and higher glycogen stores."
  - Pre- and/or post-exercise CHO+protein or protein can support strength and body composition, and "the size and timing of a pre-exercise meal may impact the extent to which post-exercise protein feeding is required."
  - Post-exercise protein "immediately to 2-h post" stimulates MPS robustly.
  - Meal frequency has limited impact on weight loss.
- **Aragon AA, Schoenfeld BJ. "Nutrient timing revisited: is there a post-exercise anabolic window?"** *JISSN* 2013;10:5. DOI [10.1186/1550-2783-10-5](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-10-5). Narrative review. OA 139 cites (CR 102). Gold OA. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/23360586)
  - Challenges the classic narrow-window view.
  - Commonly cited practical guidance from the full text, not re-verified here: the pre- and post-exercise meals should be separated by no more than about **3–4 h**, and each should contain about **0.4–0.5 g/kg** protein.
- **Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon AA, Krieger JW. "The effect of protein timing on muscle strength and hypertrophy: a meta-analysis."** *JISSN* 2013;10:53. DOI [10.1186/1550-2783-10-53](https://doi.org/10.1186/1550-2783-10-53). Meta-regression of 23 RCTs for hypertrophy and 20 for strength. OA 201 cites. Gold OA. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/24299050)
  - After controlling for covariates there was "no significant differences… for strength or hypertrophy."
  - Total protein intake was the strongest predictor.
- **Schoenfeld BJ, Aragon A, Wilborn C, Urbina SL, Hayward SE, Krieger J. "Pre- versus post-exercise protein intake has similar effects on muscular adaptations."** *PeerJ* 2017;5:e2825. DOI [10.7717/peerj.2825](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.2825). RCT, n=21 trained men, 10 weeks. OA 33 cites. Gold OA. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28070459)
  - **25 g protein** immediately pre vs immediately post training gave similar strength and hypertrophy.
  - The window "may be as wide as several hours."
- **Casuso RA, Goossens L. "Does Protein Ingestion Timing Affect Exercise-Induced Adaptations? A Systematic Review with Meta-Analysis."** *Nutrients* 2025;17(13):2070. DOI [10.3390/nu17132070](https://doi.org/10.3390/nu17132070). Meta-analysis of 5 RCTs directly comparing pre vs post, PROSPERO-registered. Gold OA. **Newest on this question.** — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/40647175)
  - No effect on lean body mass (SMD −0.08).
  - No effect on chest press RM.
  - Leg press RM favoured pre-exercise protein (SMD 0.70, p=0.048), but the subgroup difference was not significant.

### Inferences
- App rule: do not force a "30-minute shake." Schedule a protein-containing meal within about 2–3 h on either side of training. If the user trains fasted (for example early morning), prioritise eating soon after training.
- For lifters, CHO timing matters mainly when the user trains twice a day or does very high volume.

### Gaps
- Evidence on fasted vs fed resistance training performance comes mostly from small acute studies (see Henselmans 2022). There is no large RCT.

## 4. Hydration

### Takeaway
The governing documents are still ACSM 2007 and NATA 2017. Start euhydrated, keep the body-mass loss from sweat to **<2%**, individualise intake by measuring sweat rate (weigh before and after exercise), and avoid overdrinking (risk of hyponatremia).

### Cited Findings
- **American College of Sports Medicine; Sawka MN, Burke LM, Eichner ER, Maughan RJ, Montain SJ, Stachenfeld NS. "American College of Sports Medicine position stand. Exercise and fluid replacement."** *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2007;39(2):377–390. DOI [10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597](https://doi.org/10.1249/mss.0b013e31802ca597). Position stand. OA 1,496 cites (CR 1,164), the most cited paper in this list. Closed. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/17277604)
  - Goal: prevent ">2% body weight loss from water deficit."
  - Prehydrate "at least several hours before."
  - Estimate sweat rate from body-weight change.
  - Electrolyte/CHO drinks can help "under certain circumstances."
  - Commonly cited full-text values, not re-verified here:
    - Prehydration: about **5–7 mL/kg ≥4 h before**, plus **3–5 mL/kg ~2 h before** if no urine is produced or urine is dark.
    - Recovery: about **1.5 L per kg lost**.
- **McDermott BP, Anderson SA, Armstrong LE, Casa DJ, Cheuvront SN, Cooper L, Kenney WL, O'Connor FG, Roberts WO. "National Athletic Trainers' Association Position Statement: Fluid Replacement for the Physically Active."** *J Athl Train* 2017;52(9):877–895. DOI [10.4085/1062-6050-52.9.02](https://doi.org/10.4085/1062-6050-52.9.02). Position statement. OA 434 cites. Green OA (PMC5634236).
  - Quantify sweat rates.
  - Avoid both hypohydration and hyperhydration/hyponatremia.
  - Plan intake before, during and after exercise.
  - — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28985128)

### Inferences
- In-app rule: weigh before and after training. Each 1 kg lost is about 1 L of sweat; replace about 1.25–1.5 L per kg lost, with sodium if losses are large. Typical gym sessions under 60–90 min need only water to thirst unless the environment is hot.

### Gaps
- I found no updated ACSM hydration stand after 2007. The NATA full-text numbers (for example the sodium range) were not extracted because the download timed out.

## 5. Diet composition approaches (low-carb/keto vs higher-carb) for body composition

### Takeaway
ISSN 2017, ISSN 2024 (keto) and the new ISSN October 2026 stand on diets for weight reduction all agree on three points:
- When energy and protein are matched, the carbohydrate:fat ratio can be individualised to preference.
- Ketogenic diets tend to reduce fat-free mass more than higher-carb diets, partly through glycogen and water shifts.
- Keto gives similar strength gains but neutral or worse performance overall.

### Cited Findings
- **Aragon AA, Schoenfeld BJ, Wildman R, Kleiner S, VanDusseldorp T, Taylor L, Earnest CP, Arciero PJ, Wilborn C, Kalman DS, Stout JR, Willoughby DS, Campbell B, Arent SM, Bannock L, Smith-Ryan AE, Antonio J. "International society of sports nutrition position stand: diets and body composition."** *JISSN* 2017;14:16. DOI [10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12970-017-0174-y). Position stand. OA 301 cites (CR 259). Gold OA. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/28630601)
  - "A wide range of dietary approaches (low-fat to low-carbohydrate/ketogenic, and all points between) can be similarly effective for improving body composition."
  - Intermittent caloric restriction shows "no significant advantage" over daily restriction.
  - Long-term success depends on compliance.
- **NEW, partly supersedes Aragon 2017 for fat loss: Aragon A, Campbell B, Schoenfeld B, Stout J, Kalman D, Arciero P, Arent S, Kreider R, Taylor L, Bannock L, Wildman R, Kerksick C, Antonio J. "International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: diets for body weight reduction."** *JISSN* 2026;23(1):2740266, published 13 Oct 2026. DOI [10.1080/15502783.2026.2740266](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2026.2740266). Gold OA (PMC13665703). Citations ~0 (just published). The 15 points include:
  - (2) VLED (<800 kcal) and LED (800–1200 kcal) diets give no long-term advantage and may increase lean-mass loss.
  - (3) No differential effect of intermittent fasting variants (ADF, TRE, 5:2) vs continuous restriction.
  - (4) Meal frequency is not crucial.
  - (8) "The dietary carbohydrate‒fat proportion can be individualized to personal preference… the range of ketogenic to low-fat diets performs similarly."
  - (9) Ketogenic diets "tend to decrease fat-free mass to a greater degree than higher-carbohydrate comparators," especially in short trials because of glycogen and water shifts.
  - (11) Adaptive thermogenesis "is not an inevitable response."
  - (13) Plateaus are cues to practise maintenance.
  - Points 5–7 cover protein, which is out of scope here.
  - — [Europe PMC full text](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC13665703)
- **Leaf A, Rothschild JA, Sharpe TM, Sims ST, Macias CJ, Futch GG, Roberts MD, Stout JR, Ormsbee MJ, Aragon AA, Campbell BI, Arent SM, D'Agostino DP, Barrack MT, Kerksick CM, Kreider RB, Kalman DS, Antonio J. "International society of sports nutrition position stand: ketogenic diets."** *JISSN* 2024;21(1):2368167. DOI [10.1080/15502783.2024.2368167](https://doi.org/10.1080/15502783.2024.2368167). Position stand. OA 21 cites. Gold OA. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/38934469)
  - Nutritional ketosis is serum ketones **>0.5 mM**, which broadly requires **<50 g CHO/day**.
  - Keto is "largely neutral or detrimental" for athletic performance, despite fat oxidation of about 1.5 g/min.
  - Strength gains are similar to higher-CHO diets, although a minority of studies favour non-keto diets.
  - Keto may cause greater losses of body weight, fat mass and fat-free mass, likely because of calorie and protein differences and fluid shifts.
- **Ashtary-Larky D, Bagheri R, Asbaghi O, Tinsley GM, Kooti W, Abbasnezhad A, Afrisham R, Wong A. "Effects of resistance training combined with a ketogenic diet on body composition: a systematic review and meta-analysis."** *Crit Rev Food Sci Nutr* 2022;62:5717 (epub 2021). DOI [10.1080/10408398.2021.1890689](https://doi.org/10.1080/10408398.2021.1890689). Meta-analysis of 13 RCTs, n=244. OA 50 cites. Closed. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/33624538)
  - Compared with non-keto diets during resistance training, keto lowered body mass by −3.67 kg, fat mass by −2.21 kg and **fat-free mass by −1.26 kg**.
  - The negative effect on FFM is "not ameliorated by the addition of RT."
- **Vargas-Molina S, Gómez-Urquiza JL, García-Romero J, Benítez-Porres J. "Effects of the Ketogenic Diet on Muscle Hypertrophy in Resistance-Trained Men and Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis."** *IJERPH* 2022;19:12629. DOI [10.3390/ijerph191912629](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph191912629). Meta-analysis of 5 studies, n=111 trained participants. OA 17 cites. Gold OA. — [Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/36231929)
  - No significant FFM difference.
  - Keto "can be an alternative for increasing muscle mass as long as energy surplus is generated."
  - More than 8 weeks without interruption is not advisable because of satiety and adherence problems.
  - **This conflicts with Ashtary-Larky 2022.** The difference likely reflects energy-matched vs ad libitum designs and Vargas-Molina's small sample.
- A 2024 medRxiv preprint (Sinott et al.) also found a slight FFM reduction with keto plus RT. It is **not peer-reviewed**. — [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.06.19.24308878.full.pdf)

### Inferences
- App rule: offer balanced, low-carb or keto patterns as preferences. Warn that keto lowers scale weight and DXA "lean mass" early (water and glycogen), may slightly blunt lean-mass gain, and is suboptimal for high-volume or performance goals. For a hypertrophy goal, default to the higher-CHO pattern.

### Gaps
- Few long (>12 week) energy-matched, protein-matched keto vs high-CHO RCTs exist in trained lifters, especially women.

## 6. Evidence-based supplements: IOC 2018, creatine, caffeine, newer ISSN stands

### Takeaway
Only a handful of supplements have strong evidence:
- creatine monohydrate
- caffeine
- buffering agents (β-alanine, sodium bicarbonate)
- dietary nitrate
- plus protein foods and vitamin D for deficiency correction

For lifters, creatine (about **3–5 g/day**) and caffeine (**3–6 mg/kg ~60 min pre**) are the core. Recent meta-analyses (2023–2025) confirm about **+1.1 to 1.4 kg FFM** from creatine with RT, while direct hypertrophy measures show a small effect.

### Cited Findings
**IOC consensus**
- **Maughan RJ, Burke LM, Dvorak J, Larson-Meyer DE, Peeling P, Phillips SM, Rawson ES, Walsh NP, Garthe I, Geyer H, Meeusen R, van Loon LJC, Shirreffs SM, Spriet LL, Stuart M, Vernec A, Currell K, Ali VM, Budgett RG, Ljungqvist A, Mountjoy M, Pitsiladis YP, Soligard T, Erdener U, Engebretsen L. "IOC consensus statement: dietary supplements and the high-performance athlete."** *Br J Sports Med* 2018;52(7):439–455. DOI [10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2018-099027). Consensus statement. OA 1,000 cites (CR 667). Hybrid OA (PMC5867441). Co-published in *IJSNEM* 2018;28(2):104–125, DOI [10.1123/ijsnem.2018-0020](https://doi.org/10.1123/ijsnem.2018-0020), OA 536 cites. — [Europe PMC abstract](https://europepmc.org/article/MED/29540367), [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5867441/)
  - Only "caffeine, creatine, specific buffering agents and nitrate" have good evidence.
  - Do a nutritional assessment first; take a food-first approach.
  - Trial supplements in training before competition.
  - Doses from the full-text tables:
    - **Caffeine:** **3–6 mg/kg ~60 min pre**. Lower doses (<3 mg/kg, ~200 mg) can be taken before or during exercise. **≥9 mg/kg** gives no extra benefit and more side effects.
    - **Creatine:** load **~20 g/day in 4 doses for 5–7 days**, then **3–5 g/day**. Co-ingesting about 50 g CHO+protein may improve uptake. Expect **+1–2 kg body mass** (water). No negative health effects in long-term use (up to 4 years).
    - **Nitrate:** **5–9 mmol (~310–560 mg) 2–3 h pre**. The benefit plateaus above about 8.4 mmol.
    - **β-alanine:** **~65 mg/kg/day**, split into **0.8–1.6 g every 3–4 h**, for **10–12 weeks**. Gains are about 0.2–3% for efforts of 30 s–10 min. Causes paraesthesia.
    - **Sodium bicarbonate:** **0.2–0.4 g/kg 60–150 min pre**, or split over 2–4 days. GI distress is common.
    - **Vitamin D:** do not supplement indiscriminately; test first. Maintenance about **1,000 IU/day D3** from autumn to spring. Repletion protocols are physician-supervised.
  - Anti-doping: strict liability applies; prefer **third-party-tested** products.

### Inferences
- App supplement module:
  - **Tier A for lifters:** creatine monohydrate **3–5 g/day** at any time of day. Loading (**20 g/day × 5–7 days**, or 0.3 g/kg/day) is optional. Expect +1–2 kg of scale weight.
  - **Caffeine:** **3–6 mg/kg ~60 min pre** (start at 2–3 mg/kg); cap total intake and avoid late-day doses because of sleep.
  - **Situational:** β-alanine and bicarbonate (for high-rep or 30 s–10 min efforts); nitrate (mainly endurance).
  - **Not recommended for hypertrophy:** ω-3 (no hypertrophy benefit in young adults); HMB (evidence in trained lifters weak, not extracted).
  - Always flag third-party testing.

### Gaps
- No updated IOC supplement consensus after 2018 was found. No 2023–2026 update of the ISSN creatine (2017) or caffeine (2021) position stands was found in searches.
- I did not verify per-capita safety ceilings for caffeine (for example the ~400 mg/day EFSA/FDA figure) in this session.
