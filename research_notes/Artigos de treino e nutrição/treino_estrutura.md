## Rest intervals between sets

### Takeaway
Hypertrophy occurs across a wide range of rest intervals. A small benefit favors resting more than 60 s, and there is no clear extra gain beyond about 90 s for hypertrophy. Longer rest (about 2–3+ min) helps preserve volume load and strength on heavy compound lifts. The 2026 ACSM stand found no consistent strength difference between short (<1 min) and long (>1 min) rest, and hypertrophy was inconclusive. A practical default is 2–3 min for heavy multi-joint lifts and 1–2 min for single-joint and isolation work.

### Cited Findings
- **Singer et al. 2024 (Bayesian MA, 9 studies, 19 measurements):** Controlled short (≤60 s) vs longer (>60 s) rest gave SMDs of arm 0.13 (95% CrI −0.27 to 0.51; P(favoring longer)=0.74), thigh 0.17 (−0.13 to 0.43; P=0.88), and whole body −0.08. The authors conclude there is "a small hypertrophic benefit" for rest >60 s, and that the evidence for any benefit beyond 90 s is equivocal. Set end-point (failure or not) did not change the rest effect. — [Singer 2024, Front Sports Act Living](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/sports-and-active-living/articles/10.3389/fspor.2024.1429789/full)
- **Schoenfeld et al. 2016 (RCT, 21 trained men, 8 wk, 3 full-body sessions/wk, 3×8–12RM of 7 exercises):** 3-min rest produced significantly greater 1RM squat and bench and greater anterior-thigh muscle thickness than 1-min rest. Triceps showed a trend (p=0.06). — [PubMed 26605807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)
- **Grgic et al. 2017 (SR, 6 studies):** Both short (≤60 s) and long (>60 s) rest can build muscle. Newer studies in trained participants using sensitive measures suggest a possible advantage for long rest. — [PubMed 28641044](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28641044/)
- **ACSM 2026:** For strength, short (<1 min) vs long (>1 min) rest showed no difference (2 reviews, n=982, quality of evidence 63%). For hypertrophy the result was inconclusive (4 reviews, n=265, QoE 44%). — [Currier 2026, PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)
- **ACSM 2009:** For intermediate and advanced strength training, emphasize heavy loading (1–6RM) with **3–5 min rest**. For hypertrophy, use **1–2 min rest** in the 6–12RM zone. For power, use **3–5 min**. For local muscular endurance, use **<90 s**. — [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)

### Inferences
- Suggested app rule: compound or heavy lifts (squat, deadlift, bench, row, press) get **2–3 min** of rest, and **3–5 min** for strength-focused sets of ≤6 reps. Isolation and machine work gets **60–120 s**. Supersets or time-efficient plans can use about 60 s, accepting a small hypertrophy cost.
- The older ACSM 2009 advice of 1–2 min rest for hypertrophy is weakly contradicted by Schoenfeld 2016 and Singer 2024. Avoid ≤60 s as the hypertrophy default.

### Gaps
- No rest-interval meta-analysis stratified by exercise type (multi- vs single-joint) exists. The multi- vs single-joint split above is an inference.
- I did not retrieve the ACSM 2009 full-text statement of "2–3 min for core exercises" for novices. Only the abstract values above were verified.

## Exercise selection, variation and order

### Takeaway
Exercise order matters for strength: whatever is trained first improves most. It does not matter for hypertrophy. Put the exercise the user most wants to progress first, usually a multi-joint lift. Varying exercises systematically (different angles or muscle lengths) can improve regional hypertrophy, but rotating exercises randomly or too often may hurt gains. Free weights vs machines makes no strength difference.

### Cited Findings
- **Nunes et al. 2021 (MA, 11 studies):** Multi-joint (MJ) strength gains favored starting with MJ exercises (ES=0.32, p=0.034). Single-joint (SJ) strength gains favored starting with SJ exercises (ES=−0.58, p=0.032). Order had no effect on hypertrophy (ES=0.03, p=0.862). Conclusion: "increases in muscular strength are the largest in the exercises performed at the beginning of an exercise session." — [PubMed 32077380](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32077380/)
- **ACSM 2026:** Placing an exercise at the beginning of the session favored strength (4 reviews, n=941, QoE 88%). Order had no effect on hypertrophy (4 reviews, n=759, QoE 81%). Free weights vs machines showed no strength difference (n=683, QoE 75%). Varied selection and single- vs multi-joint were inconclusive. — [PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)
- **ACSM 2009:** Sequence exercises "large before small muscle group exercises, multiple-joint exercises before single-joint exercises, and higher-intensity before lower-intensity exercises". Include bilateral and unilateral, single- and multi-joint exercises. — [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)
- **Kassiano et al. 2022 (SR, 8 studies, n=241 young men):** "Some degree of systematic variation seems to enhance regional hypertrophic adaptations and maximize dynamic strength, whereas excessive, random variation may compromise muscular gains". Redundant exercises or a high frequency of exercise change "may actually hinder" adaptations. — [PubMed 35438660](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35438660/)
- **ACSM 2026 (press release):** Machines vs free weights, training to failure, and complex periodization "did not consistently impact outcomes for the average healthy adult". Bands, bodyweight and home training are effective. — [ACSM 2026 update page](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/)

### Inferences
- Plan-generation rules: (1) put the priority lift (usually MJ) first. (2) Choose 2+ exercises per muscle group that differ biomechanically (for example, an overhead triceps extension plus a pushdown). (3) Keep the core exercises stable across a mesocycle of about 4–8 weeks rather than changing them each session. (4) Let equipment availability decide machines vs free weights.

### Gaps
- There are no robust data on the optimal number of exercises per muscle per session, or on exact rotation intervals. The "4–8 week" figure is an inference, not a tested value.

## Range of motion (ROM) and lengthened partials

### Takeaway
Full ROM is the evidence-based default. It is reliably better for strength and at least as good for hypertrophy, and lower-limb hypertrophy favors full ROM in Pallarés 2021. Training at long muscle lengths (lengthened partials, or exercises that stretch the muscle, such as seated leg curl) may give a small hypertrophy edge, but the latest meta-analysis (Varovic 2025) finds the effects on regional hypertrophy trivial. Avoid short-length (shortened) partials as the main stimulus.

### Cited Findings
- **Pallarés et al. 2021 (MA, 16 studies, RoB 2/GRADE):** Full ROM beat partial ROM for strength (ES=0.56, p=0.004) and lower-limb hypertrophy (ES=0.88, p=0.027). Functional performance favored full ROM without reaching significance (ES=0.44). Muscle architecture showed no difference. — [PubMed 34170576](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34170576/)
- **Wolf et al. 2023 (MA):** The pooled SMD of 0.12 (95% CI −0.02 to 0.26) favored full ROM, which is trivial. Lengthened partials vs full ROM for hypertrophy gave SMD −0.28 (−0.81 to 0.16), meaning lengthened partials were possibly better but imprecise. Conclusion: "using a full or long ROM may enhance results for most outcomes", and partial ROM "might present an efficacious alternative for variation and personal preference" or for injury constraints. — [Wolf 2023, IJSC](https://journal.iusca.org/index.php/Journal/article/view/182)
- **Varovic et al. 2025 (Bayesian MA, 12 studies):** Longer vs shorter mean muscle length had trivial effects on regional hypertrophy: mid-belly SMD 0.07 (95% QI −0.02 to 0.15), distal 0.09 (−0.01 to 0.19), with a trend increasing toward distal sites. "RT at both longer and shorter mean muscle lengths produces similar hypertrophic effects". A caveat is that the conditions differed by only about 21.8% in mean muscle length. — [PubMed 40570881](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40570881/)
- **Maeo et al. 2021 (within-subject RCT, n=20, 12 wk, 5×10 at 70% 1RM, 2×/wk):** Seated leg curl (hamstrings lengthened) grew the whole hamstrings more than prone leg curl (+14% vs +9% MRI volume). Biarticular hamstrings gained +8–24% vs +4–19%. — [PubMed 33009197](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33009197/)
- **ACSM 2026:** Full ROM favored strength (2 reviews, n=1,262, QoE 50%). Hypertrophy was inconclusive (1 review favoring full ROM, 3 inconclusive). — [PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)

### Inferences
- App default: prescribe full ROM. Prefer exercise variants that load the muscle at long lengths (seated over prone leg curl, overhead triceps extension, incline curls, deep squats). Lengthened partials can be offered as an intensity technique, for example on the final set or for calves, but should not be the default.
- Wolf 2023 vs Varovic 2025 conflict only mildly. Both say the long-length advantage is at most small.

### Gaps
- A 2025 systematic review on longitudinal growth (Wolf et al., *Sports Med Health Sci*) is reported in secondary sources to favor long-length training. I did not verify its DOI or abstract, so it is excluded.

## Periodization (linear vs undulating vs non-periodized)

### Takeaway
When volume is equated, periodization helps **maximal strength** modestly (ES about 0.3–0.4), and undulating periodization beats linear in **trained** lifters only. Periodization does **not** affect hypertrophy. For the average healthy adult, ACSM 2026 says complex periodization is not necessary. Simple progression with some planned variation is enough.

### Cited Findings
- **Moesgaard et al. 2022 (MA, 35 volume-equated studies):** Periodized vs non-periodized training favored periodization for 1RM (ES 0.31, 95% CI 0.04–0.57). Hypertrophy did not differ (ES 0.13, −0.10 to 0.36). Undulating (UP) vs linear (LP) favored UP for 1RM (ES 0.31, 0.02–0.61), but only in trained participants (ES 0.61). Untrained participants showed no difference (ES 0.06). LP vs UP did not differ for hypertrophy (ES 0.05). — [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)
- **Williams et al. 2017 (MA, 18 studies, 81 ES):** Periodized vs non-periodized training favored periodization for 1RM (ES 0.43, 95% CI 0.27–0.58). Undulating programs were more favorable. Gains were larger in untrained people and with higher frequency and longer duration. Note that this partly conflicts with Moesgaard on training status, and Williams did not require equated volume. — [PubMed 28497285](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/)
- **ACSM 2026:** Periodization had no effect on hypertrophy (4 reviews, n=1,414, QoE 63%). Strength was inconclusive, with one review slightly favoring periodization under volume-equated conditions. — [PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)
- **ACSM 2009:** Intermediate and advanced lifters should use 1–12RM "in a periodized fashion". When the lifter can do 1–2 reps over the target, increase the load by 2–10%. — [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)

### Inferences
- App logic: beginners and hypertrophy goals get simple linear progression (double progression), with no complex periodization needed. Trained users with strength goals get daily or weekly undulating periodization (for example a heavy day with 3–6 reps, a moderate day with 6–10, and a light day with 10–15).

### Gaps
- There is little evidence on block periodization in non-athletes, and neither MA analyzed it.

## Training splits (full-body vs split)

### Takeaway
When weekly volume is equated, split and full-body routines produce equivalent strength and hypertrophy. Choose based on schedule and preference.

### Cited Findings
- **Ramos-Campo et al. 2024 (MA, 14 studies, n=392):** Bench press MD 1.19 kg (−1.28 to 3.65, p=0.34). Lower-limb strength MD 2.47 (p=0.29). Hypertrophy showed no difference for elbow extensors, elbow flexors, vastus lateralis or lean mass (all p>0.8). "Individuals are free to confidently select a resistance training routine based on their personal preferences." — [PubMed 38595233](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38595233/)
- **ACSM 2026:** Recommends training all major muscle groups at least 2×/week. Training 1 vs >5 days/week made no hypertrophy difference when volume was equated (n=3,177, QoE 63%). — [PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)
- **ACSM 2009:** Frequency of 2–3 d/wk for novices, 3–4 for intermediates and 4–5 for advanced lifters. — [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)

### Inferences
- App mapping: 2–3 days/week → full-body. 4 days → upper/lower. 5–6 days → push/pull/legs or upper/lower plus extras. Each structure should still hit every muscle at least 2×/week, per ACSM 2026.

### Gaps
- There are few long-term studies (>12 wk) of splits in women and older adults.

## Deloads

### Takeaway
The evidence is weak and mostly expert opinion. Experts define a deload as a planned or autoregulated period of reduced training stress, usually achieved by cutting volume (sets, reps, exercises), with intensity held or slightly lowered and greater distance from failure. It is not complete cessation. Stopping training entirely for 1 week did not hurt hypertrophy in trained lifters but did blunt strength.

### Cited Findings
- **Bell et al. 2023 (Delphi, 3 rounds, 21–34 expert coaches, consensus ≥70%):** Definition: "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training." Agreed principles: a deload can be included in each mesocycle (100%). It can be pre-planned, autoregulated, or both (100%). Reduce volume by lowering sets or reps or cutting training days (100%) or exercises (95%). Intensity can stay the same while volume drops (95.2%). Reduce proximity to failure (100%). There was no consensus on a fixed interval or length. — [Bell 2023, PMC10511399](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10511399/)
- **Coleman et al. 2024 (RCT, 39 trained men and women, 9-wk high-volume program, 1-wk full cessation at the midpoint):** The deload group showed no difference in lower-body hypertrophy, power or endurance. The continuous group gained more isometric and dynamic lower-body strength. — [PubMed 38274324](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38274324/)
- **ACSM 2026:** Deloads were not addressed. — [PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)

### Inferences
- App rule: every 4–8 weeks, or when readiness or performance drops, schedule a deload week at about 40–60% of normal sets, keep the loads, and stop 3+ reps short of failure. Prefer this "active deload" over full rest, because full rest costs some strength (Coleman 2024). The 4–8 week and 40–60% figures are inferences from practice, not trial-validated.

### Gaps
- No RCT compares deload frequency or the reduced-volume deload format. Coleman tested only full cessation.

## Beginners vs trained individuals

### Takeaway
Most evidence comes from untrained people. Beginners respond to almost any sensible program, so simple full-body routines 2–3×/week with moderate loads and linear progression are enough. Trained people benefit more from finer manipulations such as undulating periodization for strength and longer rests.

### Cited Findings
- **ACSM 2009:** Definitions: novice means no RT experience or none for several years. Intermediate means about 6 months of consistent RT. Advanced means years of RT. Novices: 8–12RM loads, 2–3 d/wk. Intermediate and advanced: 1–12RM periodized, 3–4 and 4–5 d/wk respectively. — [PubMed 19204579](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19204579/)
- **ACSM 2026:** Most evidence came from inexperienced trainees. A prior analysis found that training experience had minimal effect on strength and hypertrophy. Only a few variables can be manipulated to enhance adaptation in experienced trainees. — [PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)
- **Moesgaard 2022:** The undulating-periodization advantage appears only in trained participants (ES 0.61 vs 0.06 untrained). — [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/)
- **Williams 2017:** The periodization benefit for 1RM was greater in untrained people (β=−0.59), so findings on training-status moderation are inconsistent. — [PubMed 28497285](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28497285/)
- **Grgic 2017 and Schoenfeld 2016:** The long-rest advantage is clearest in trained lifters. — [PubMed 28641044](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28641044/), [PubMed 26605807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26605807/)

### Inferences
- Use ACSM 2009's novice, intermediate and advanced tiers (<6 mo, 6 mo to about 2 yr, multi-year) as the app's onboarding classification. Escalate complexity (undulating periodization, more exercise variety, intensity techniques) only for intermediate and advanced users.

### Gaps
- No direct meta-analysis compares program structures in trained vs untrained people beyond the subgroup analyses above.

## General physical-activity guidelines (WHO 2020, ACSM 2011, ACSM 2026)

### Takeaway
The baseline weekly target for every adult plan is **150–300 min of moderate or 75–150 min of vigorous aerobic activity** (or an equivalent mix), **plus muscle-strengthening work for all major muscle groups on at least 2 days**, and less sedentary time. Older adults should add multicomponent balance and strength training on at least 3 days/week. ACSM 2011 adds flexibility work (≥2 d/wk, 60 s per stretch in total) and neuromotor training (2–3 d/wk).

### Cited Findings
- **WHO 2020:** "All adults should undertake 150–300 min of moderate-intensity, or 75–150 min of vigorous-intensity physical activity, or some equivalent combination … per week." It recommends regular muscle-strengthening activity for all age groups and reducing sedentary behaviour, with no quantified threshold. "Some physical activity is better than none." — [PubMed 33239350](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33239350/)
- **WHO 2020 full text:** Adults should do moderate-or-greater-intensity muscle-strengthening activities involving all major muscle groups on **≥2 days/week**. Adults ≥65 should do varied multicomponent activity (functional balance and strength) at moderate or greater intensity on **≥3 days/week**. Going beyond 300 min moderate or 150 min vigorous brings additional benefits. — [BJSM full text PDF](https://bjsm.bmj.com/content/bjsports/54/24/1451.full.pdf) (these specifics come from the guideline tables, not the abstract)
- **ACSM 2011 (Garber):** Moderate cardio for ≥30 min/day on ≥5 d/wk (≥150 min/wk), or vigorous for ≥20 min/day on ≥3 d/wk (≥75 min/wk), or a combination reaching ≥500–1000 MET·min/wk. Resistance exercise for each major muscle group on 2–3 d/wk, plus neuromotor exercise (balance, agility, coordination). Flexibility for each major muscle-tendon group on ≥2 d/wk, totaling 60 s per exercise. Break up sedentary time. Below-target amounts still help. — [PubMed 21694556](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21694556/)
- **ACSM 2026:** Train all major muscle groups at least 2×/week. The main goal for most adults is regular participation in any RT. Programs should be individualized for goals, enjoyment and safety. — [ACSM 2026 update](https://acsm.org/resistance-training-guidelines-update-2026/); [PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)

### Inferences
- Every generated weekly plan should be checked against WHO and ACSM minimums: at least 150 min of moderate aerobic activity or equivalent, at least 2 RT days covering all major muscle groups, and for users ≥65, balance work on at least 3 days.

### Gaps
- I did not verify the ACSM 2011 full-text RT details (2–4 sets, 8–12 reps, ≥48 h between sessions for the same muscle group). They are widely quoted but not in the abstract.

## Cardio / concurrent training interference with hypertrophy

### Takeaway
The current best evidence (Schumann 2022, 43 studies) shows concurrent aerobic plus strength training does **not** compromise hypertrophy or maximal strength in healthy adults. It does attenuate **explosive strength**, especially when both are done in the same session. The older Wilson 2012 meta-analysis found interference that scaled with running, frequency and duration. Practical mitigation: separate sessions by at least 3 h (ideally different days), prefer cycling or low-impact cardio, and keep cardio volume moderate.

### Cited Findings
- **Schumann et al. 2022 (MA, 43 studies, supervised ≥4 wk):** Concurrent vs strength-only SMDs were maximal strength −0.06 (−0.20 to 0.09, p=0.446), explosive strength −0.28 (−0.48 to −0.08, p=0.007), and hypertrophy −0.01 (−0.16 to 0.18, p=0.919). Explosive-strength attenuation was larger in the same session (p=0.043) and not significant when sessions were ≥3 h apart. Cycling vs running, >5 vs <5 sessions/week, training status and age had no moderating effect. — [PubMed 34757594](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34757594/)
- **Wilson et al. 2012 (MA, 21 studies, 422 ES):** Hypertrophy ES was 1.23 for strength-only, 0.85 for concurrent and 0.27 for endurance-only. Strength ES was 1.76, 1.44 and 0.78; power ES was 0.91, 0.55 and 0.11. Running, but not cycling, caused significant decrements in hypertrophy and strength. Endurance frequency (r=−0.26 to −0.35) and duration (r=−0.29 to −0.75) correlated negatively with outcomes. — [PubMed 22002517](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22002517/)
- **ACSM 2026:** Concurrent training effects were inconclusive for strength, hypertrophy and power (3 reviews each, QoE 67–75%). — [PMC12965823](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12965823)
- Schumann 2022 supersedes Wilson 2012 on the hypertrophy question. It is newer, has twice the studies and required identical strength prescriptions. Wilson's modality and duration signals remain useful as conservative design heuristics.

### Inferences
- App rules: (1) Hypertrophy users can safely meet the WHO 150–300 min aerobic target. (2) When cardio and lifting share a day, put lifting first or separate them by at least 3 h, especially for power or athletic goals. (3) Favor cycling, incline walking or rowing over high-volume running for hypertrophy-focused users. (4) Keep sessions moderate (about 20–30 min). This duration is inferred from Wilson's negative duration correlation, not a tested threshold.

### Gaps
- Evidence in well-trained lifters doing high aerobic volumes is limited. Schumann's samples were mostly untrained or moderately active, so the "no interference" conclusion may not generalize to advanced bodybuilders.
- I did not search further for 2023–2026 concurrent-training meta-analyses beyond ACSM 2026's umbrella. A newer MA may exist that I did not check.
