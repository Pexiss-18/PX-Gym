## Q1. Which meta-analyses define the dose-response of weekly volume (sets per muscle per week)?

### Takeaway
More weekly sets produce more hypertrophy, with diminishing returns that grow stronger at higher volumes. The minimum effective dose is about 4 sets/muscle/week. Efficiency is highest between 5 and 10 sets. Gains keep coming, but more slowly, at about 11–20+ sets. The newest paper (Pelland 2025/26) finds no clear plateau or inverted U up to about 30–40 sets, but data above about 25 sets is sparse. For strength, volume shows a strong plateau after about 4–5 weekly sets. Pelland 2025/26 is the current reference and supersedes Schoenfeld 2017 for volume.

### Cited Findings
- **Pelland 2025/26 design:** 67 studies, 2058 participants (79.1% male, mean age about 25 years). Models were adjusted for intervention duration and training status. Indirect sets were counted with the "fractional" method, which had the strongest evidence: a set where the muscle is a synergist (e.g., triceps in bench press) counts as 0.5. — [Pelland 2025, Sports Med (abstract via Europe PMC)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)
- **Pelland: volume and hypertrophy.** The posterior probability that the slope is above 0 was 100% for both hypertrophy and strength. Both models show diminishing returns, and they are "considerably more pronounced" for strength. — [Pelland 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)
- **Pelland hypertrophy efficiency tiers (fractional weekly sets per muscle)** — [Pelland preprint, Table 2A](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.460):
  - The minimum effective dose is **4 sets**.
  - "Higher efficiency" is **5–10 sets**, where about 6 more sets bring another detectable gain.
  - "Intermediate" is **11–18 sets**, needing about 8.5 more sets.
  - "Lower" is **19–29 sets**, needing about 10.75 more sets.
  - "Lowest" is **30–42 sets**, needing about 12.5 more sets.
  - **43+ sets** is unclear (too little data).
  - The smallest detectable effect is 2.05% muscle size.
  - These numbers come from the preprint. The published version may have changed slightly; check before quoting.
- **Pelland: marginal effect per set.** Each additional set adds about **0.24% hypertrophy** (95% CrI 0.15–0.33%) at the mean volume of about 12 sets. This compares with about 0.37–0.38% per set in Schoenfeld 2017. The best-fit model was a square root curve, meaning accelerating diminishing returns but *no inverted U*. Few studies go beyond about 25 sets. — [Pelland preprint](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.460)
- **Pelland: volume and strength.** The best-fit model was reciprocal, with a "functional plateau." The minimum effective dose is **1 fractional set/week**. Detectable increments stop at about **4 sets/week**, against a smallest detectable effect of 3.96%. — [Pelland preprint, Table 2B](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.460)
- **Schoenfeld 2017:** 15 studies, 34 groups. **Each extra weekly set added ES +0.023, about +0.37% muscle growth.** Higher vs lower volume differed by ES 0.241, about **3.9 percentage points**. Comparing the categories <5, 5–9 and 10+ sets/muscle/week showed only a trend (p=0.074). The result is a "graded dose-response." — [Schoenfeld 2017, J Sports Sci](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197)
- **Schoenfeld 2017, as summarized by Pelland:** ≥9 sets/muscle/week gave ES 0.46 vs ES 0.32 for <9 sets (non-significant). The authors could not draw conclusions above about 9 sets because of limited data. — [Pelland preprint, intro](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.460)
- **Baz-Valle 2022:** trained men aged 18–35, ≥1 year of training. Moderate volume (12–20 sets) and high volume (>20 sets) did not differ for the quadriceps (p=0.19) or biceps (p=0.59). Triceps favoured >20 sets (p=0.01). The authors suggest **12–20 weekly sets per muscle** as an optimal standard recommendation. — [Baz-Valle 2022, J Hum Kinet](https://doi.org/10.2478/hukin-2022-0017)
- **Ralston 2017 (strength):** high weekly sets beat low weekly sets (≤5 sets/exercise/week) with ES diff 0.18 (95% CI 0.06–0.30, p=0.003). Medium sets beat low sets with ES diff 0.15 (p=0.04). This holds for both multi-joint and isolation exercises. — [Ralston 2017, Sports Med](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-017-0762-7)
- **Currier 2023 (BJSM NMA):** the highest-ranked hypertrophy prescription was **multiset, higher load, 2×/week** (SMD 0.66 vs control). The highest-ranked prescriptions for hypertrophy all included multiple sets. — [Currier 2023, BJSM](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807)

### Inferences
- Practical default for the app's hypertrophy plans:
  - **Beginners:** about 6–10 fractional sets/muscle/week.
  - **Intermediate:** about 10–20 sets.
  - **Advanced:** up to about 20–30 sets, applied conservatively because of fatigue and time cost.
  - Count synergist sets as 0.5.
  - This is my synthesis of Pelland's tiers and Baz-Valle, not a recommendation either paper states directly.
- For strength goals, extra volume beyond about 5 sets/lift/week adds little. Load and frequency/practice matter more.

### Gaps
- The published Pelland version was paywalled, so I used the preprint's tier numbers. They may differ slightly in the final paper.
- Pelland mentions a "parallel project" on per-session volume (whether there is a cap on useful sets per session). I did not locate or verify it.

## Q2. Frequency, load, and proximity to failure

### Takeaway
- **Frequency:** when weekly volume is equated, frequency barely affects hypertrophy, so use frequency to distribute volume (≥2×/week per muscle is a sensible default). Frequency does help strength (Pelland 2025/26).
- **Load:** hypertrophy is similar across a wide range of loads (about 30% to >85% 1RM, or roughly 6–30+ reps) when sets are taken close to failure. Heavy loads (>80% 1RM, ≤8RM) are superior for 1RM strength.
- **Proximity to failure:** for hypertrophy, closer to failure gives somewhat more growth (Robinson 2024), but momentary failure is not required (Refalo 2023; Grgic 2022). For strength, RIR barely matters.

### Cited Findings — Frequency
- **Schoenfeld 2019:** 25 studies. With volume equated there was no significant difference between higher and lower frequency, including in trained subjects and for upper and lower body. In non-volume-equated studies there was a modest effect favouring 3+ days vs 1 day/week. The authors conclude frequency "does not significantly or meaningfully impact muscle hypertrophy when volume is equated" and "can be chosen based on personal preference." — [Schoenfeld 2019, J Sports Sci](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906)
- **Pelland 2025/26 (frequency and hypertrophy):** the posterior probability was below 100% (91.3% in the preprint), so the result is compatible with a negligible effect. The direct-effects analysis showed no effect. — [Pelland 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w); [preprint](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.460)
- **Pelland 2025/26 (frequency and strength):** the posterior probability was 100%, a positive effect with diminishing returns. Estimated strength gain was 12.7% at 1 fractional session/week vs 17.3% at 2 sessions/week, with "accelerating diminishing returns" beyond that. — [Pelland preprint](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.460)
- **Grgic 2018 (strength):** 22 studies. ES rose with frequency: 0.74 at 1×/week, 0.82 at 2×, 0.93 at 3× and 1.08 at 4+× (p=0.003). With volume equated the effect was not significant (p=0.421). In this analysis frequency acts mainly as a way to add volume. — [Grgic 2018, Sports Med](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-018-0872-x). Pelland's newer non-linear model partly contradicts this, since it finds an independent frequency effect on strength. — [Pelland preprint](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.460)
- **Schoenfeld 2016 (older):** 10 studies. Twice a week beat once a week (ES 0.49 vs 0.30, p=0.002). The authors inferred that major muscles should be trained ≥2×/week. — [Schoenfeld 2016, Sports Med](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8). The 2019 analysis with more data superseded this.
- **Currier 2023:** the top-ranked prescription for strength was higher load, multiset, **3×/week** (SMD 1.60 vs control). For hypertrophy it was higher load, multiset, 2×/week (SMD 0.66). — [Currier 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807)

### Cited Findings — Load / rep ranges
- **Schoenfeld 2017 (load):** 21 studies, all sets to momentary failure, low load (≤60% 1RM) vs high load (>60% 1RM). 1RM gains were greater with high load. Isometric strength did not differ. **Hypertrophy was similar** between conditions. The authors conclude hypertrophy "can be equally achieved across a spectrum of loading ranges." — [Schoenfeld 2017, JSCR](https://doi.org/10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200)
- **López 2021 (network meta-analysis):** 28 studies, n=747, sets to failure, comparing low load (>15RM), moderate load (9–15RM) and high load (≤8RM). Hypertrophy did not differ between loads (p=0.11–0.47). Strength gains were larger for high load vs low (SMD 0.60–0.63) and moderate vs low (SMD 0.34–0.35). High vs moderate leaned towards high (SMD 0.26–0.28, p=0.068). — [López 2021, MSSE](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002585)
- **Currier 2023:** "Higher-load (>80% 1RM) prescriptions maximised strength gains, and all prescriptions comparably promoted muscle hypertrophy." — [Currier 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807)
- **Schoenfeld 2021 (repetition continuum re-examined):** the authors propose a new paradigm in which hypertrophy can be obtained, and sometimes optimized, "across a wide spectrum of loading zones." This contradicts the classic view of heavy load for strength, moderate load for hypertrophy and light load for endurance. — [Schoenfeld 2021, Sports](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports9020032)

### Cited Findings — Proximity to failure (RIR/RPE)
- **Robinson 2024:** meta-regressions on estimated RIR, adjusted for load, volume equating, duration and training status. For **hypertrophy**, the slope for RIR was negative with a CI excluding 0, meaning "muscle hypertrophy improves as sets are terminated closer to failure." For **strength**, the CI included 0, meaning "strength gains were similar across a wide range of RIR." The authors call the analysis exploratory and note the RIR values were estimated. — [Robinson 2024, Sports Med](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2)
- **Refalo 2023:** 15 studies. Any definition of set failure vs non-failure gave a trivial advantage (ES 0.19, 95% CI 0.00–0.37, p=0.045). Momentary muscular failure vs non-failure showed **no advantage** (ES 0.12, CI −0.13 to 0.37). A velocity loss threshold >25% vs 20–25% showed none either (ES 0.08). The authors suggest a **non-linear** relationship between proximity to failure and hypertrophy. — [Refalo 2023, Sports Med](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y)
- **Grgic 2022:** 15 studies. Failure vs non-failure showed no difference for strength (ES −0.09) or hypertrophy (ES 0.22, CI −0.11 to 0.55). In non-volume-equated studies, **non-failure training was better for strength** (ES −0.32). In trained subjects, **failure was slightly better for hypertrophy** (ES 0.15, CI 0.03–0.26). — [Grgic 2022, JSHS](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007)
- **Refalo 2024 (RCT, trained, within-subject):** over 8 weeks, leg press/leg extension to failure and the same exercises at 2/1 RIR produced the same quadriceps growth (0.181 vs 0.182 cm). Failure caused more velocity and repetition loss, i.e., more fatigue. — [Refalo 2024, J Sports Sci](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021)

### Inferences
- **Load:** for hypertrophy the app can prescribe any rep range from about 6 to 30 reps, provided sets end at about 0–3 RIR. For strength, include ≥80% 1RM work (≤6–8RM).
- **Failure:** a reasonable default is 1–3 RIR on most sets, with failure reserved for the last set or for isolation and machine exercises. This combines Robinson's dose-response with Refalo's "failure not required" and the fatigue findings. Light loads (>15 reps) need to be close to failure to work, because the López and Schoenfeld load studies all used sets to failure.
- **Frequency:** ≥2 sessions/muscle/week mostly helps by distributing volume. For strength goals, more frequent practice of the main lifts helps (2–3×/week).

### Gaps
- No good meta-analysis was found on rest intervals or per-session volume caps (out of scope, but relevant to the AI).
- The RIR values in Robinson 2024 are retrospective estimates, so there is no precise "optimal RIR" figure.

## Q3. Which papers inform how to progress load over time (beginners vs trained)?

### Takeaway
The ACSM 2009 position stand remains the only authoritative, explicitly quantified progression model, and it is by far the most cited paper here. Its key rule: raise load **2–10%** once the lifter can do **1–2 reps beyond** the target. It also gives frequency and loading levels by training status. Plotkin 2022 is a trained-subject RCT showing that progressing reps at a fixed load works about as well as progressing load. No newer meta-analysis supersedes the ACSM model. Note that ACSM's hypertrophy rep ranges (6–12RM) are outdated in light of the load meta-analyses above.

### Cited Findings
- **ACSM 2009, novices** (no RT experience): loads of **8–12RM**, frequency **2–3 days/week**. — [ACSM 2009, MSSE](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670)
- **ACSM 2009, intermediate and advanced** (about 6 months, or years, of training): a wider **1–12RM** range in a periodized fashion, eventually emphasizing **1–6RM** with 3–5 min rest for strength. Frequency is **3–4 days/week** for intermediates and **4–5 days/week** for advanced. — [ACSM 2009](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670)
- **ACSM 2009, progression rule:** "a 2–10% increase in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number." — [ACSM 2009](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670)
- **ACSM 2009, hypertrophy:** 1–12RM, periodized, with emphasis on **6–12RM**, 1–2 min rest, and "higher volume, multiple-set programs." For local muscular endurance: 40–60% 1RM for more than 15 reps with less than 90 s rest. — [ACSM 2009](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0b013e3181915670)
- **Plotkin 2022 (RCT):** 43 trained subjects (≥1 year of training), 8 weeks, 4 sets × 4 lower-body exercises, 2×/week. The LOAD group raised load and kept reps fixed; the REPS group raised reps and kept load fixed. Rectus femoris growth slightly favoured REPS (+2.8 mm, CI −0.5 to 5.8). Squat 1RM slightly favoured LOAD (+2.0 kg, CI −2.4 to 7.8). Neither difference was clearly practically significant. "Both progressions of repetitions and load appear to be viable strategies." — [Plotkin 2022, PeerJ](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.14142)
- **López 2021:** untrained subjects showed greater hypertrophy. In those with training experience, more RT sessions gave better gains (p=0.031–0.045). This supports progressing volume and frequency as training status rises. — [López 2021](https://doi.org/10.1249/MSS.0000000000002585)
- **Kraemer & Ratamess 2004:** the foundational review of progression and prescription that the 2009 stand builds on. — [Kraemer & Ratamess 2004, MSSE](https://doi.org/10.1249/01.MSS.0000121945.36635.61)

### Inferences
- For the app's progression engine, a double-progression model fits both papers. Within a rep range (e.g., 8–12), add reps until the top of the range plus 1–2 reps is reached at the target RIR, then raise load 2–10%. Use the smaller increments (about 2–5%) for upper body and isolation exercises. The increment split is a common practical interpretation, not stated in the abstract.
- For trained users, repetition progression is a valid alternative when load jumps are impractical, such as with dumbbells or machines with coarse plate steps.

### Gaps
- I found no meta-analysis comparing periodization or progression models (linear vs undulating vs autoregulated RPE) within the scope or tool budget. Candidate areas to check separately: periodization meta-analyses, and autoregulation (RPE/velocity vs %1RM) reviews.
- The ACSM 2009 stand has not been officially updated. I found no newer ACSM resistance-training progression position stand through 2026.

## Q4. Recent (2023–2026) papers that supersede older ones

### Takeaway
- **Pelland 2025/26** supersedes Schoenfeld 2017 on volume and both Schoenfeld 2016/2019 and Grgic 2018 on frequency.
- **Robinson 2024** refines Refalo 2023 and Grgic 2022 on failure into a continuous dose-response.
- **Currier 2023** is the broadest single synthesis: 178 strength RCTs and 119 hypertrophy RCTs, with combined load × sets × frequency prescriptions.

### Cited Findings
- Pelland: "volume and frequency have unique dose-response relationships with each hypertrophy and strength gains." The volume–strength relationship has more pronounced diminishing returns. Only strength shows a consistent frequency effect. — [Pelland 2025](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)
- Pelland's frequency–strength result is "in contradiction to previous meta-analyses" such as Grgic 2018. The authors attribute this to non-linear models and the fractional set counting method. — [Pelland preprint](https://doi.org/10.51224/srxiv.460)
- Robinson 2024 contrasts with Refalo 2023's "non-linear / failure not superior" conclusion. Robinson finds a continuous benefit of lower RIR for hypertrophy but none for strength. — [Robinson 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-024-02069-2); [Refalo 2023](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y). The two can be reconciled: training close to failure matters, while reaching *momentary failure* itself adds little (supported by the Refalo 2024 RCT, [link](https://doi.org/10.1080/02640414.2024.2321021)).
- Currier 2023 confirms that load does not matter for hypertrophy but heavy loads (>80% 1RM) matter for strength, and that multiple sets matter for hypertrophy. — [Currier 2023](https://doi.org/10.1136/bjsports-2023-106807)

### Inferences
- The knowledge base should list Pelland 2025/26, Robinson 2024 and Currier 2023 as primary sources and the older Schoenfeld meta-analyses as corroborating context.
- Open-access options at comparable quality: Currier 2023, López 2021, Refalo 2023, Robinson 2024, Grgic 2022, Plotkin 2022, Schoenfeld 2021, Ralston 2017, Baz-Valle 2022. Pelland is available through the SportRxiv preprint.

### Gaps
- Google Scholar counts were not retrieved. Every count here is from OpenAlex.
- I did not search for 2026 umbrella reviews or new meta-analyses beyond Pelland. A follow-up search may find more, e.g., on per-session volume or sex differences.
